# Paper Before Machine

A person could follow the Logic Theorist by hand, Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon wrote, but only “barely.” Their 1956 paper described a system for discovering proofs in symbolic logic. It did not report the system's computer realization: that was explicitly outside its scope. What could a description of a reasoning system accomplish, if a person could follow it only with such difficulty?

The answer depended on what the description contained. Newell and Simon set out to identify all the processes involved and establish how they interacted. A reader could, in principle, work through the specified activity. That made the system more than a suggestion that a machine might reason. It also exposed a burden: a procedure could be precise enough to inspect without being easy to carry out by hand.

Proof discovery was the particular activity they chose. The system relied heavily on heuristic methods, which the authors related to methods observed in human problem solving. Their interest extended beyond the result to the processes by which it was reached. They stated a broader research tactic: construct complex information-processing systems, then study their structure and behavior empirically. Logic Theorist gave that tactic a specific system to describe and examine.

Such a description was a substantial undertaking. Each process had to fit with the others in an account definite enough to follow. The word “barely” measures the difficulty of following it, not evidence of a particular hand simulation. It also leaves a question open: how could the specified activity be carried out when the work exceeded what was convenient for a person?

Newell and Simon treated physical realization as a distinct problem. Once a system was specified, a corresponding physical system had to be found or built. Depending on the case, they wrote, realization could be easy or insurmountable. The distinction matters: a precise description says what a system is to do; it does not by itself provide a physical system that does it.

Their acknowledgment connected that problem to the collaboration. They credited J. C. Shaw as a partner in many parts of the enterprise, singling out his undertaking to realize Logic Theorist in a computer, and referred readers to subsequent papers for that work. The acknowledgment points toward an implementation while the paper itself concentrates on the specification. It does not tell us when a computer actually ran the system.

The authors looked to the high-speed digital computer as a means of realization. In their account, it could realize an exceptionally wide range of information-processing systems; the practical limits lay in speed and memory rather than in the complexity of the processes. That was an argument about capability, not a report of a run. A machine offered a way to carry out processes beyond a convenient human workload, if those processes could be expressed in a form the machine could accept.

But the form mattered to people too. Newell and Simon argued that machine code was poorly suited to human thought and discussion about complex systems. An account written only for the machine would make the system harder to discuss; an account meant for people still had to be connected to a machine. Pseudo-code, or an interpretive language, offered a bridge: a suitable program could interpret a description that people could also read. The language used to state the processes was therefore part of the problem of realizing them.

The paper made a careful distinction about that language. The logic language displayed in its pages had not been coded for a computer. A closely related language, less convenient for exposition, was being coded. One served the explanation in the paper; work on a related one was directed toward computer use. Neither statement supplies a date or an account of a completed run.

The result is a paper poised between two demands. Its system had to be explicit enough for a reader to follow, even if only barely; it also pointed toward a physical realization that was a separate task. The authors' proposed language joined those demands without making them identical. Before turning to how Logic Theorist searched for proofs, we can see why setting down the search required care: the processes had to be intelligible together, and their description had to leave open a way for a computer to carry them out.
