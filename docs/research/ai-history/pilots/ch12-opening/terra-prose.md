# Paper Before Machine

How much of a proof-finding machine could be made definite on paper? In their 1956 paper, Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon described one that was just small enough for a person to simulate by hand. Their word was “barely.” Its task was to discover proofs in elementary symbolic logic. They called the system Logic Theorist, or LT, and the language that specified it the logic language, or LL. The distinction matters: one was the system whose behavior they wanted to describe; the other was the means of saying what that behavior was.

There was more than one way to approach the task. Newell and Simon pointed to algorithms available for the same problems and to a physical realization of one algorithm, the Kalin-Burkhart machine. LT, by contrast, relied heavily on heuristic methods, which the authors related to methods observed in human problem solving. The comparison placed two approaches beside each other: an algorithmic method and a system built around heuristics, both directed at the same kind of problem.

To make LT definite enough to examine, the authors had to specify every process and exactly how the processes interacted. They described that work as lengthy and arduous. A name for a method was not enough; the account had to say how its parts fit together. The detail served their stated research tactic: construct complex information-processing systems and study their structure and behavior empirically. LT was a particular system whose organization could be set out and which was just small enough to simulate by hand.

Then came a different problem. A description could specify the behavior of a system; realization meant finding or constructing a physical system with that behavior. How hard that would be depended on the system. Newell and Simon offered a contrast from electrical circuitry: circuitry for linear differential equations was relatively easy to find, while circuitry representing most kinds of nonlinear systems was difficult. The example gives the word “realization” its force. A clear account of the behavior required does not settle how to produce it physically.

The authors looked toward the high-speed digital computer. In their account, it could realize almost any information-processing system, subject to limits in overall speed and memory rather than the complexity of its processes. Their opening summary defined the paper's scope as specification rather than computer realization. They credited J. C. Shaw as a partner in many aspects of the enterprise, particularly for undertaking to realize LT in a computer; they wrote that this work would be reported in subsequent papers.

A computer could offer a physical realization, but it also raised a question about language. Machine code was suitable for communicating with a computer, Newell and Simon argued, but not at all suitable for people thinking and communicating about complex systems. The people describing LT needed to understand and discuss its processes. The computer needed a description it could interpret. How could one account serve both purposes?

Their proposed answer was pseudo-code, or an interpretive language. It would be comprehensible to people, while a suitable program could enable a computer to interpret it. In this approach, specification and realization came together under the single task of describing the system in appropriate pseudo-code. The choice of language was not merely a choice of how to present an already finished account: it connected the form in which people could discuss the system to a means by which a computer could interpret it.

The language displayed in the paper had its own status. LL had not been coded for a computer; a very similar language, less convenient for exposition, was in the process of being coded. The two forms answered the demands of exposition and computer use differently. For the reader, LL supplied a way into LT: the language made the system's processes available for examination.

LL began with elementary information processes and combined them to specify more complex ones. Generally speaking, the inputs and outputs of its elementary processes were symbolized information. The next question is what that information looks like in the language and how processes can receive, change and pass it on. To understand the machine on paper, we first have to read the symbols through which LL describes its activity.
