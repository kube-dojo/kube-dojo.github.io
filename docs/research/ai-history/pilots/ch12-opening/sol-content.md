# Paper Before Machine

Allen Newell and Herbert A. Simon placed the Logic Theorist just inside the range of systems a person could simulate by hand. Their parenthetical "barely" marks that edge: small enough, just. The system's task was to discover proofs in elementary symbolic logic. They called the system LT and the language used to specify it the logic language, LL. How much of a proof-finding machine could be made definite on paper? The distinction between the system and its language gives that question a practical starting point.

Proof discovery already offered a comparison. Newell and Simon pointed to available algorithms for the same tasks and to a physical realization of one of them, the Kalin-Burkhart machine. LT relied heavily on heuristic methods, which they related to methods observed in human problem solving. Their stated contrast was between algorithmic and heuristic approaches to the same problem-solving tasks. A machine's ability to reach a proof was therefore only part of the comparison. Different approaches to the task could be examined through the systems that embodied them.

For LT, such an examination required a specification of every process and of exactly how the processes interacted. A list of promising ideas would leave too much unsettled. The authors described the task of specifying the system as lengthy and arduous; the detailed account was itself part of the work. It served their stated research tactic: construct complex information-processing systems, then study their structure and behavior empirically. LT supplied one particular system whose organization could be made explicit. Its hand-simulable scale put following that organization within human reach.

Giving the system a precise description left a physical problem to solve. Newell and Simon called finding or constructing a physical system with the specified behavior realization. To show how widely the difficulty could vary, they compared electrical circuitry for linear differential equations with circuitry for nonlinear systems. The first was relatively easy to find; representing most kinds of the second was difficult. The example puts substance into the gap between description and construction. Having equations or procedures available does not settle how a physical system will carry out the required behavior.

Their research looked toward the high-speed digital computer. In the authors' account, it could realize almost any information-processing system, with limits in overall speed and memory rather than in the complexity of its processes. In the opening summary, they defined this paper's scope as specification rather than computer realization. They also credited J. C. Shaw as a partner in many aspects of the enterprise, especially his undertaking to realize LT in a computer. That work, they wrote, would be reported in subsequent papers. The formal account belonged to a collaboration also working toward its physical realization.

The computer brought the language problem into focus. Machine code was suitable for communicating with the computer, Newell and Simon argued, but unsuitable for people thinking and communicating about complex systems. The researchers needed to discuss the processes they were specifying, while giving a computer a way to carry them out. Writing entirely at the machine's level would make the first task harder. A language chosen only for readable exposition would still need a route to computer execution. The form of the specification had to answer to both demands.

Their proposed connection was pseudo-code, or an interpretive language: a description comprehensible to people that a computer could interpret through a suitable program. In this approach, they brought specification and realization together under the single task of describing the system in appropriate pseudo-code. The language was doing more than making a technical report easier to read. Its form could help connect the description of a complex system to a means of studying its behavior. The two problems met in the work of stating the processes.

The particular LL displayed in the paper had not been coded for a computer. A very similar language, less convenient for exposition, was in the process of being coded. That difference shows the demands pulling on the language: explaining a system clearly and preparing a description for computer use could favor different forms. LL was the form the reader could examine. LT was the particular problem-solving system being specified through it. Keeping those names distinct lets the discussion move from the promise of a machine to the language needed to describe its activity.

LL began with elementary information processes. More complex processes were to be specified by combining those elementary ones. Generally speaking, the inputs and outputs of the elementary processes consisted of symbolized information. This gives the next step a concrete direction. To follow LT, we need to follow what its language allows a process to receive, change and pass on, and how combinations of processes make up the larger system. The proof-finding machine on paper now asks to be read through LL, beginning with the information its processes operate on.
